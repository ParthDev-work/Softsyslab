Software Development
Company Website
Product Requirements Document
Version 1.0 | 4 October 2026
This document defines a substantial corporate website for a custom software development and digital
product engineering company. It provides the scope, content, interaction rules, data contracts,
publishing controls and release tests needed to design, build and operate the website. The
recommended implementation is a modular Next.js website with a managed editorial CMS and a
separate, small operational database for enquiries. The planned information architecture contains 79
named routes or reusable detail templates. Publication depends on genuine capabilities and approved
content, not a page-count target.
The business owner supplies and approves the company facts identified in brackets. Proposed
operating practices and sample copy become publishable only after the responsible owner confirms
that the company actually follows them. A professional website supports transparent evaluation; it
does not guarantee bank, payment-provider or vendor approval.

1 Document Overview
Audience: business owner, product manager, designer, frontend and backend engineers, content
editor, SEO specialist, QA engineer, security reviewer and operations owner.
Status: implementation specification with business-fact dependencies. The document recommends
product decisions; it does not establish that any particular legal entity, service capability, certification,
employee or customer exists.
Document conventions: MUST is a launch acceptance requirement when its feature is in scope;
SHOULD is the recommended implementation; MAY is optional. P0 means required for launch, P1
means the next planned release and P2 means a separately funded future feature. A P0 conditional
feature is required only when the corresponding business activity is offered. REQ identifiers and AC
identifiers support ticket and test traceability.
Approvals: product owner approves scope; engineering lead approves architecture; design lead
approves accessible layouts; business owner verifies claims; counsel approves applicable policies;
security lead approves controls; QA owns release evidence. One person may fill several roles, but
each responsibility must have a named owner.
Change control: maintain a versioned decision log with decision, date, owner, rationale, affected
requirements and acceptance tests. Changes to public company information, tracking, uploads,
authentication or commercial terms require the relevant owner to review the change before
publication.
2 Executive Summary
Build a website that lets a buyer answer three questions: can this company handle my problem, how
will the work be delivered, and what happens if I start a conversation? Organize the offer into product
strategy and design; application engineering; AI and integration; cloud and modernization; and
quality, support and teams.
Prioritize substantive service pages, transparent engagement terms, an understandable development
process and reliable enquiry handling. Use real project evidence where available. When evidence is
absent, explain deliverables and decisions using clearly labelled illustrative diagrams rather than
invented client outcomes.
P0 includes the full reusable design system, core corporate and policy pages, approved service pages,
content management, contact flow, security, accessibility and monitoring. The 79-route plan is the
supported target architecture, not a requirement to publish unsupported capabilities. P1 adds the
guided enquiry, richer editorial search and additional verified sector content. P2 includes the client
portal and multilingual content.

6 Target Audience
Primary audiences are founders, product owners and technology leaders with a concrete software
initiative. Secondary audiences include operations, marketing technology, procurement, job seekers
and delivery partners. Initial service and industry priorities must follow actual sales demand and staff
capability.
Primary journeys: problem → solution → service → process → enquiry; technical evaluation →
technology → security → case evidence → technical discussion; commercial evaluation → engagement
models → ownership and support → enquiry; due diligence → company identity → policies → contact;
candidate → careers → genuine vacancy → application.
7 Buyer Personas
Startup founder
Problem: uncertain scope, constrained runway and limited technical capacity. Motivation: validate a
useful first release before committing to a large build. Objections: scope inflation, lock-in and unclear
ownership. Decision criteria: discovery outputs, staged investment and maintainability. Expected
information: what an MVP includes, exclusions, founder responsibilities and handover. CTA: “Plan Your
MVP.” Confidence content: sample discovery agenda, acceptance checklist and a real early-stage
project when available.
SaaS founder
Problem: subscriptions, onboarding, tenancy and product iteration compete for attention. Motivation:
launch or improve a recurring-use product. Objections: unreliable billing, weak tenant isolation and
growing infrastructure costs. Criteria: entitlement design, migration approach and release process.
Expected information: architecture options, integrations, maintenance and operational costs. CTA:
“Discuss Your SaaS Product.” Confidence content: tenancy decision guide and verified SaaS
architecture narrative.
Small business owner
Problem: disconnected tools and recurring manual work. Motivation: make one important workflow
easier. Objections: custom software cost and dependence on a supplier. Criteria: build-versus-buy
advice, understandable scope and ongoing costs. Expected information: practical examples, training,
support and who owns accounts. CTA: “Discuss Your Workflow.” Confidence content: workflow map
and plain-English engagement explanation.
SME decision maker
Problem: departments use inconsistent data and undocumented processes. Motivation: improve
coordination while protecting day-to-day operations. Objections: migration disruption and employee
adoption. Criteria: phased rollout, permissions and training. Expected information: integration
planning, milestones and stakeholder participation. CTA: “Plan a Business System.” Confidence
content: migration checklist and acceptance workshop outline.

Enterprise technology team
Problem: dependencies, vendor governance and integration complexity. Motivation: add delivery
capacity for a defined initiative. Objections: procurement friction, data exposure and unsupported
architecture. Criteria: documented interfaces, testing evidence and auditability. Expected information:
delivery governance, security practices and contractual responsibilities. CTA: “Discuss Enterprise
Delivery.” Confidence content: example documentation contents and security contact.
CTO
Problem: architecture trade-offs and technical debt under delivery pressure. Motivation: obtain sound
engineering decisions and an accountable partner. Objections: opaque code quality and technology
hype. Criteria: architecture rationale, review process and maintainability. Expected information: stack
selection, CI/CD, observability and ownership. CTA: “Discuss the Architecture.” Confidence content:
annotated reference architecture clearly identified as illustrative.
CIO
Problem: portfolio cost, supplier risk and fragmented systems. Motivation: create a manageable
modernization programme. Objections: business interruption and incomplete governance. Criteria:
phased investment, operational ownership and risk management. Expected information: delivery
model, business continuity and escalation. CTA: “Plan a Modernization Discussion.” Confidence
content: dependency map and change governance example.
Head of engineering
Problem: hiring constraints, release bottlenecks and inconsistent contributions. Motivation: strengthen
delivery without losing technical control. Objections: onboarding overhead and quality variance.
Criteria: verified skills, review standards and availability. Expected information: team structure,
overlap hours and replacement process. CTA: “Discuss Team Capacity.” Confidence content:
onboarding plan and actual role profiles.
Product manager
Problem: stakeholder disagreement and unclear acceptance criteria. Motivation: turn user needs into
a sequenced release. Objections: missed requirements and late design changes. Criteria: discovery,
backlog clarity and feedback cadence. Expected information: user stories, demos, UAT and change
handling. CTA: “Plan Product Discovery.” Confidence content: sample story and release acceptance
template.
Digital transformation manager
Problem: legacy processes resist organization-wide change. Motivation: modernize a measurable
business workflow. Objections: technology-first proposals and poor adoption. Criteria: process
research, incremental deployment and training. Expected information: current-state mapping,
stakeholder plan and migration controls. CTA: “Map Your Transformation.” Confidence content:
roadmap example with business dependencies.

Marketing technology team
Problem: fragmented CRM, forms, attribution and content systems. Motivation: connect tools and
improve reliable data flow. Objections: tracking compliance and broken campaigns. Criteria:

Operations team
Problem: repetitive entry, exception queues and spreadsheet dependence. Motivation: reduce
avoidable manual handling. Objections: automation failures and loss of control. Criteria: approvals,
human fallback and audit logs. Expected information: triggers, failure handling and access rules. CTA:
“Review an Operational Workflow.” Confidence content: exception-handling flow and administrator

White-label agency
Problem: client commitments exceed engineering capacity. Motivation: obtain dependable delivery
under agreed branding. Objections: confidentiality, client ownership and inconsistent communications.
Criteria: non-solicitation terms if agreed, escalation and handover standards. Expected information:
collaboration model, contracting entity and boundaries. CTA: “Discuss Delivery Partnership.”
Confidence content: sample roles and responsibilities matrix; no fictional partnership logos.
Buyer of dedicated developers
Problem: sustained capacity gap. Motivation: add specific skills without building an entire local team.
Objections: hidden subcontracting, unverified CVs and turnover. Criteria: actual experience,
availability and transparent management. Expected information: interview process, replacement
notice and billing basis. CTA: “Discuss Developer Requirements.” Confidence content: verified profiles
only and onboarding checklist.
Legacy software owner
Problem: fragile releases, unsupported dependencies and inaccessible business logic. Motivation:
improve the system while keeping it operational. Objections: big-bang rewrites and data loss. Criteria:
assessment quality, incremental migration and reversibility. Expected information: discovery access,
test strategy and cutover planning. CTA: “Review Your Existing System.” Confidence content:
modernization decision tree and rollback plan example.
8 Brand Positioning
Positioning: Custom Software Development & Digital Product Engineering Partner.
Core statement: “Software built around your business.” Supporting copy: “Plan, design and develop
software around the work your users need to do. From a first product release to an existing platform,

maintain. Avoid “world-class,” “best,” “guaranteed,” arbitrary client counts and unsupported
“enterprise-grade” claims.

10 Information Architecture
Services describe work the company performs. Solutions describe systems buyers need. Industries
describe sector-specific workflows and constraints. Technologies explain implementation choices.
These must not become near-identical pages with substituted keywords.
Use 14 principal templates: homepage, category hub, service, solution, industry, technology category,
case-study listing, case-study detail, corporate/process, conversion form, resource listing,
article/guide, careers/job and policy/trust. Jobs and applications share a page shell but separate
validation. Reuse components without forcing every page into the same visual rhythm.
Every published page must have a useful onward path: parent hub, relevant related page and
contextual contact CTA. Critical policy pages must remain reachable directly from the footer.
Breadcrumbs show canonical hierarchy, not a visitor’s click history.

12 Navigation Architecture
Desktop navigation uses two visual levels to accommodate the requested architecture. Main row: logo
to Home, Services, Solutions, Industries, Technologies, Work and Resources; primary button “Discuss
Your Project” to /contact/. Utility row: How We Work, Company, Careers and Contact. Company
dropdown includes Business Information, Engagement Models, Support and Security. A secondary
“View Our Work” link appears only if approved work exists; otherwise use “Explore Our Process.”
At narrower laptop widths collapse to a menu before labels wrap. All eleven requested navigation
destinations remain accessible in mobile navigation; never place an unavailable destination in the
menu. Mega menus have a category heading, two or three short groups and a “View all” link. Click
and keyboard activation are supported; hover is an enhancement. Escape closes and restores focus;
Tab follows normal document order. Do not assign application-menu ARIA roles to ordinary site
navigation.
Footer groups: Company, Services, Solutions, Industries and Technologies, Resources, Legal, Contact.
Show full legal name and accurate address label, business email, supported phone, copyright year
and verified active social URLs. Use real text links, not icon-only contact methods. On mobile, link
groups may be accordions with accessible buttons; identity, contact and legal links stay readily
visible.

56 Acceptance Criteria
AC01 Navigation. Given published destinations, when a visitor uses desktop or mobile navigation by
keyboard, then every displayed link works, the menu can be dismissed and focus returns predictably.
With no approved work, the Work link and associated CTA are omitted or replaced by Process, not
routed to an empty gallery.
AC02 Publication truth. Given a record containing an unresolved placeholder, unapproved claim or
missing customer permission, when publication is attempted, then the CMS blocks it and explains the
missing approval. It cannot appear in public HTML, API, sitemap, search or schema. Given permission
withdrawal, unpublishing removes all references and cached public versions within the configured
invalidation target of five minutes.
AC03 Technical evaluation. Given a CTO evaluation task, when the visitor opens a service and follows
relevant links, then architecture decisions, testing, deployment and maintenance boundaries are
available in no more than three navigation choices. The page names only verified capabilities.
AC04 Commercial clarity. Given a proposed engagement, when the visitor reads Engagement Models,
then billing basis, scope handling, change approval, ownership boundaries and support references are
explicit, and no unapproved price or binding promise appears.
AC05 Company consistency. Given a verified legal name/address/contact change, when settings are
published, then footer, contact, company information and structured data use the new approved
value, and policy references are reviewed for consistency. Registered and operating addresses retain
correct labels.
AC06 Valid enquiry. Given valid required fields and acknowledgment, when Submit is activated, then
one durable submission, related lead and outbox jobs are committed before a receipt is shown. The
receipt contains a non-sensitive reference and next steps.
AC07 Invalid enquiry. Given missing or invalid values, when Submit is activated, then no lead is saved,
focus reaches an accessible error summary, each error links to its field and valid entries remain
present. Errors never require color recognition alone.
